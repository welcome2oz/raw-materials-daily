**HEADLINE:** Brazil's plastics converters brace for drought costs as Rio Negro plunges near Manaus

**PUBLISHED:** 01-Oct-2026

**ATTRIBUTION:** Jonathan Lopez

**BODY:**

SAO PAULO (ICIS)–Brazil's plastics converters in the Manaus industrial hub are building inventories and bringing production forward as a fast-intensifying drought threatens to choke river access for resin supplies, according to trade group Abiplast and the plastics trade unions federation Simplast.

Rio Negro falls 5.21m in September, low water already considered severe. Severe drought could cut vessel capacity by 55%, lift transport costs up to 195%. Abiplast/Simplast warn drought costs could be partly passed on to consumer goods prices.

"The prospect of another severe drought is a concern for the plastics processing industry, especially given the likelihood of the El Nino phenomenon intensifying," Abiplast said in a written response to ICIS.

The Rio Negro dropped 5.21m in Manaus during September, from 24.09m on 1 September to 18.88m on the last day of that month, according to Port of Manaus data. The decline accelerated to as much as 23cm/day between 19-25 September.

That leaves the river close to 17.7m, the level at which waterways regulator Antaq's tacit approval for the so-called drought surcharge kicks in this year.

"The primary risk lies in restrictions on the navigability of Amazonian rivers; these constraints hinder ship access, reduce cargo transport capacity, and can cause delays in the supply of resins and other raw materials used by the Manaus Industrial Hub," said Abiplast/Simplast.

Manaus is home to Brazil's free trade zone and a major manufacturing center for electronics, home appliances and air conditioners.

It is also a key entry point for imported chemicals and polymers, including polyethylene (PE), with coastal shipping moving around 60% of cargo into the zone.

Researchers from the Brazilian Geological Survey (SGB) and the National Institute for Amazonian Research (INPA) said this year's low water season could be among the three most severe on record, surpassed only by 2023 and 2024, which also greatly affected petrochemicals players in the area.

In the scenarios analyzed, the Rio Negro could fall to between 13-16m at Manaus, with the most severe estimate pointing to 12.92m.

Earlier SGB calculations, based on critical years such as 2015, 2023 and 2024, put the level at the end of October at between 12.31-16.50m. Even the upper end of that range is far below what is considered healthy for the time of year.

In 2023, the Port of Manaus recorded its lowest water level in 121 years, at 13.59m in mid-October, forcing several shipping lines to suspend services altogether.

According to technical indicators from Simplast, a moderate drought that reduces vessel draft by 2m can cut transport capacity by 35% and lift container freight costs by 63%.

In a severe drought, with a 3.2m reduction in draft, cargo capacity losses could reach 55%, while unit transport costs could rise by 147-195%.

"According to technical indicators from Simplast, the cost of container transport – which normally hovers around $2,100 – can reach between $5,000 and $5,500 during critical drought periods," said Abiplast/Simplast.

To cope, companies are bringing production forward and building strategic inventories.

"While this measure helps cope with the most critical drought periods, it also requires greater working capital, increases storage costs, and places additional financial strain on companies," the two groups added.

Alternative logistics such as barges and supporting port infrastructure help keep goods moving but add transport stages and new operating costs, they said.

Abiplast/Simplast said the drought surcharge applied by shipping lines compounded other costs, including the need to carry smaller volumes per trip, carry out extra transshipments and receive raw materials earlier.

Brazil's National Waterway Transportation Agency (ANTAQ) now requires shipping companies to obtain prior approval before applying the charge, providing technical and economic evidence linking river conditions to their costs.

Cabotage trade group ABAC has argued the surcharge is justified and that ANTAQ's data requirements are unworkable.

"For the industry, it is crucial that any extraordinary charges be based on clear technical criteria and linked to costs actually resulting from navigation restrictions, thereby avoiding additional pressure on the competitiveness of companies located in the region," Abiplast/Simplast said.

The groups warned that higher costs could feed through the supply chain to the prices of goods made in Manaus.

"There is, therefore, a genuine concern that these costs will be passed on – even if only partially – along the supply chain, affecting the prices of consumer goods manufactured in the region, such as electronics, home appliances, and air conditioners," it said.

"The challenge lies in ensuring the continuity of supply and industrial production while preventing extraordinary costs caused by the drought from undermining company competitiveness or placing an excessive financial burden on Brazilian consumers."
